# Making a Home

A comfortable, well-kept home lifts your mood and lets you wake well rested. When you fall asleep under a roof, the room around you is judged. You are told how it rated when you wake, and what it still lacks.

## What makes a room comfortable

A room is the roofed space around you, closed in by walls, doors and windows. Each of these counts once, so a variety of things matters more than a lot of one thing:

| Comfort | What counts |
| --- | --- |
| A covered floor | Wood, stone, marble, tiles or carpet over at least half the floor |
| Finished walls | At least half the walls limewashed or painted (see below) |
| A light | A lamp, lantern, brazier, torch stand or fireplace |
| A bed, a seat and a table | All three; a bedroll is not a bed |
| A work of art | A painting, a statue or rock art, in the room or on its walls |
| A living plant | A filled plant pot or flowers |
| A named house | A custom sign with a name on it, on a wall or inside |
| A clean room | No more than a couple of stains or bits of rubbish |
| Sound walls | No cracked or badly weathered walls |

The room rates as **bare**, **cosy**, **comfortable**, **fine** or **splendid** depending on how many it has. All nine make it splendid.

Stand in a room and use **Judge This Room** (IC tab) to see how it would rate and what is missing.

## What a good home does

- **Mood.** A cosy or better home lifts your resting mood more than a plain roof does. It counts all day after you sleep there, like a good bed.
- **A higher ceiling.** A fine or splendid home lets your mood settle a little higher than living conditions alone otherwise allow.
- **Well rested.** Waking in a comfortable, fine or splendid home leaves you well rested for half an hour, 45 minutes or an hour. You get your breath back faster and grow hungry and thirsty more slowly.

## Upkeep

Keep the room clean and the walls in repair, and renew the wash on the walls now and then. Rubbish, stains and cracked walls each cost the room a point until you deal with them. Walls weather over the years and can be repaired with their building material (see **[Construction](Guide_to_Construction.md)**).

## Limewash, paint and stain

1. Burn stone or marble in a **lime kiln** to get **quicklime** (see **[Pottery and Lime](Guide_to_Pottery_and_Lime.md)**).
2. Fill a bucket or another open container with at least 20 units of water, then use a lump of quicklime on it. The quicklime slakes the water into 20 units of white **limewash**.
3. To colour it, use a **pigment** on the container (see **[Painting](Guide_to_Painting.md)**). This tints all the limewash in it and uses up one use of the pigment.
4. Use the container on a built wall or a laid floor. Each wall or floor tile takes 5 units.

On walls and stone floors it is a wash or paint. On wooden floors it soaks in as a stain, and plain limewash gives limed, pale wood. Roads, bridges, carpets, thatch and mats cannot be coated.

A coat lasts about two weeks. After that it looks worn and patchy, and the walls no longer count as finished until you give them a fresh coat.

## Dyed furniture and curtains

- When you pad a bed, chair, sofa or stool with **cloth**, you choose a dye for it. Leather padding keeps its own colour.
- To change the colour later, use another sheet of cloth on the padding and pick a new dye.
- Use a sheet of cloth on a cloth curtain to dye it.

The dyes are the same ones you know for tailoring. Weaving gives the natural dyes. Classical Dress and Fine Garments add more, and Textile Mills lets you mix any colour.
