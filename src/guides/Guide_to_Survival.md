# Winter and Long Rounds

## Staying warm

Cover exposed body parts and wear a coat over ordinary clothing. Overlapping layers add insulation, while shorts and loincloths protect only the parts they cover. Ordinary clothing's cold rating is **240 K**, about **-33 C**; it stops insulating below that temperature. Choose suitable cold-weather equipment for extreme climates.

Rain and immersion make you wet, weakening clothing insulation and increasing cooling. See [Rain and wet clothes](#rain-and-wet-clothes) for gear that keeps you dry. Standing under a pitched canopy or a lean-to keeps the rain off and blocks the wind, so in cold weather it feels up to 5 C warmer, though never warmer than 15 C. A nearby lit brazier, campfire, iron furnace or fireplace provides warmth. Warmth dries you faster and helps recovery from hypothermia; shelter alone can still be cold.

Complete coverage buys time but does not make you immune to cold. A dangerously cold body takes harm even when fully covered. Seek warmth before exposure becomes severe, and account for other injuries and hunger.

## Storms

Weather comes as storms that travel across the land with the wind. Rain, snow or blowing sand falls only under the storm, so one valley can be soaked while the next is dry, and you can walk out from under a storm or wait for it to pass.

- **Seeing one coming.** If a storm is heading your way while you are outdoors, you are told which direction it is coming from. The cloud's shadow darkens the ground a little before the rain reaches you.
- **What falls** depends on the land. The same storm rains on temperate ground, snows on the tundra and raises sand in the desert. It is light at the edge and heaviest in the middle.
- **Severe storms** have a core of extreme weather: a blizzard in cold lands, a monsoon in the jungle, a sandstorm in the desert. Get under a roof before the core arrives. Blizzards and sandstorms pass on with the storm.
- **Heat waves** settle over a place for a while and move on. You get thirstier, crops need more water and there is no dew. You are told when one arrives and when it has passed.
- **Lightning and thunder.** Heavy rain storms bring lightning. You see the sky flash and hear the thunder a moment later, later the further away it struck. Lightning does not hurt you or start fires.
- **Wind** rises under a storm and can blow an open umbrella inside out. The wind shifts by a quarter turn at a time and never swings right round at once.

**Reading the weather ahead.** Three things tell you what is coming before the sky does:

- A **weather vane** (6 soft wood, Primitive Construction) stands on the ground outdoors and swings to point into the wind. Examine it to learn where the wind is from, how strong it is, and whether the sky upwind is dark. It does nothing under a roof.
- A **barometer** (8 iron, Optics & Instruments) fits in a pocket. Use it in your hand: a steady needle means no change for a quarter of an hour, a falling needle means weather is on its way and tells you in how many minutes, and a needle dropping fast means a severe storm. Under a storm it tells you how long until it passes.
- The **farmer's almanac** has an Outlook line with the same forecast.

A forecast supposes the wind holds. If the wind shifts, the storm can turn away, or toward you.

Roofs keep all of it off. Rain barrels fill, crops are watered and fires are doused only where the rain is actually falling.

## Rain and wet clothes

Standing in the open during rain slowly soaks you, faster in heavy rain or a monsoon. Being indoors, under a pitched canopy or under a lean-to keeps the rain off entirely. Wet clothing insulates less, and on settlement maps soaked clothes also slow you down.

**Rain gear.** Your coat or cape, your hat and any cape fastened over your clothes each keep off part of the rain, and they stack. Ordinary clothes help a little; dedicated gear helps a lot. No combination keeps you perfectly dry forever.

| Gear | Made from | Research | Keeps off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lean-to (shelter) | wood | Primitive Construction | all rain while you are under it |
| Straw rain cape | leaves | Weaving | 70% |
| Oiled paper umbrella | bamboo | Papermaking | 70% while open |
| Sou'wester (hat) | cloth | Haberdashery | 50% |
| Oilskin coat | cloth | Textile Mills | 85% |
| Black umbrella | cloth | Textile Mills | 80% while open |
| Folding umbrella | plastic | Synthetic Fabrics | 85% while open |

Fur coats, ponchos, capes and straw hats also shed some rain. Examine a garment to see how well it keeps rain out.

**Umbrellas.** Use an umbrella in hand to open or close it. Held open in either hand, it keeps most rain off, and in hot sun it gives shade that makes the heat a few degrees easier to bear. A gale can blow an open umbrella inside out; use it in hand to bend it back.

**Greasing clothes.** Use a container holding at least 5 units of lard, olive oil or corn oil on a coat, hat or cape to rub the grease in. Lard makes it shed 60% of the rain, and plant oil 45%, until enough rain has washed the grease out.

**Capes over clothing.** Use a cape, such as the straw rain cape or a red or blue cape, on your uniform, coat or armor to fasten it over that piece. A fastened cape adds its rain protection and warmth. Remove it with the Remove Accessory verb to get the same cape back. A coat or suit of armor holds one cape.

**Drying off.** Out of the rain you dry slowly in the cold and faster in warm air. Standing within two tiles of a lit fire dries you about three times faster. The **Wring Out Clothes** verb (IC tab) takes a few seconds and leaves soaked clothes only damp. It does nothing while you are still standing in water.

**Heavy armor.** Plate armor and heavy body armor slow you down. Each ballistic plate slid into a plate carrier adds a little more weight.
## Footwear for the ground and weather

What is on your feet changes how fast you cross snow, mud and water, and how well you keep your footing. Examine a pair of shoes to see what it is good for.

| Footwear | Made from | Good for | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Snowshoes | Wood (Stone Age) | Walk on top of deep snow at nearly full speed; warm | Clumsy on bare ground, poor in mud, unsteady on steep slopes |
| Mukluks | Leather (Stone Age) | Faster in snow, warm, fair grip on ice | Soak up mud and water |
| Hobnailed boots | Leather (Iron Smithing) | Never slip on ice, sure footing on steep slopes, keep feet dry | A little slower than light shoes |
| Pattens | Wood (Tailoring) | Lift you clear of mud and puddles, keep feet dry | Wobbly on slopes, no grip on ice |
| Rubber boots | Plastic (Ready-Made Clothing) | Best in mud and wading, keep feet dry | Little grip on ice, not insulated |
| Mountaineering boots | Steel (Alloys) | Crampons never slip on ice, best on steep slopes, good in snow, warm | Heavy |

Older footwear counts too. Fur, wool and winter boots are warm and help in snow. Leather, riding, work and military boots shed water and help a little in mud. Geta lift you out of mud. Sandals, flip-flops and slippers are light, but in snow your toes freeze as if barefoot.

**Ice.** Running on frozen water can make you slip and fall. Walking never does. Boots with a good grip halve the chance, and hobnails or crampons stop it entirely.

**Cold, wet feet.** When you are already cold, standing in water or mud chills your feet unless your footwear is waterproof, and snow freezes bare or open-toed feet.

**Hills.** Running down a steep slope can trip you. Hobnails and crampons make that much less likely; snowshoes and pattens make it more likely.

## Clearing snow

Fallen snow on grass can be scooped up with a shovel into a snow pile, leaving the grass underneath. Once the snow is gone, a shovel digs up the grass as usual.

## Daylight and weather transitions

Day/night lighting and outdoor weather appearances update progressively across ticks to reduce interruptions. A visible transition may take longer to finish. If another update arrives during a transition, the latest pending request replaces the older target.

Indoor and map-level lighting exclusions, climate and seasonal weather rules, radiation appearances, and Wasteland Night/Morning effects still apply. These appearance updates do not change season lengths or advance debris age.

## Fighting fires

A fire burns its tile for a short while, hurts and ignites anyone standing in it, and keeps spreading to neighbouring grass and wooden floors until it is put out or runs out of things to burn. Bare dirt, sand, stone and water do not carry it. Rain helps put fires out. Act early: one burning tile is easy to stop, a long burning front is not.

- **Fire beater**: a leafy branch made from 2 wood with Primitive Construction knowledge. Hold it and click a fire next to you to beat at it. About half the swings put the fire out.
- **Fire flapper**: a hide flap on a pole made from 3 leather with the same knowledge. It works the same way with better odds and lasts about three times as long.
- **Soak it first**: click open water, a well, or an open container holding water with the beater or flapper in hand. A wet tool rarely fails and wears far more slowly, but it dries out after several swings, so soak it again. A dry tool scorches and falls apart quickly.
- **Someone is burning**: click them with the beater or flapper (not on harm intent) to beat the flames off. A burning person can also drop and roll with Resist.
- **Nothing to hand**: a pelt, leather or cloth held in your hand can smother a fire when you click it. It often fails, and a piece may scorch away.
- **Water**: click a fire with an open container to empty its water onto it. The more water, the surer: a cupful rarely works, half a bucket always does, and a full bucket also splashes the fires on the tiles around it and puts out anyone burning on that tile. Refill at a well or open water and keep a line of buckets moving.
- **Wet blanket**: soak a bedsheet in open water, a well or a container of water. Thrown over a fire it usually smothers it, and wrapped around a burning person it always puts them out. One soaking lasts four uses. A dry sheet works poorly and may burn up.
- **Fire hook**: a long pole with an iron hook, made from 3 iron with Iron Tools knowledge. Click a thatch or timber roof, wall or floor, or a bush or tall grass, to pull it down and leave a gap the fire cannot cross. Clicking the ground under a roof reaches for the roof. With a fire within three tiles this takes a few seconds; with no fire near it takes three times as long. A wall or floor that was not yet burning gives back half its material. Stone, brick, tile and trees cannot be pulled down.
- **Hand pump fire engine**: a water tub on wheels with a pump and a short hose, built from 20 wood with Wagons & Ships knowledge. Pour water into it from buckets (it holds 400 units), pull it close to the fire, then click it with an empty hand to take the nozzle. Click where the water should land, up to five tiles away. Each jet uses 20 water, puts out the fire on the tile it lands on and anyone burning there, and often the fires on the tiles around it. Walls stop the jet. The nozzle reaches three tiles from the engine: go further, drop it or have the engine pulled away and it returns to the engine. Click the engine with the nozzle to hang it back, and examine the engine to see how many jets are left.
- **Fire hose reel**: a hose drum built from 10 steel with Machined Tools knowledge. Build it right beside a sink or a well, which is where its water comes from; a puddle or a dried-up sink will not do. Take the nozzle and spray exactly as with the hand pump engine. The hose reaches seven tiles from the reel, and it keeps spraying for as long as the sink or well has water.
- **Fire extinguisher**: made from 7 steel with Machined Tools knowledge, and empty until you fill it with water at a sink or well. Hold it with your other hand free and click toward the fire: each burst puts out every fire and every burning person or animal on the three tiles in front of you, and walls stop it. A full one holds twelve bursts; examine it to see how many are left.

Stand beside the fire, never in it, and work along the edge that is moving toward what you want to save.

## Abandoned debris

Discarded rubbish and empty, unrecoverable spent casings become eligible to decay after **4 actual season changes** on maps with changing seasons. Examine them after **2 changes** to see the warning. Maps without an active seasonal cycle instead warn after **6 hours** and allow decay after **12 hours of running-server time**. Decay processing can take additional time after the threshold.

Pick up or move eligible debris for a fresh recovery window. Native bags, backpacks, ammunition storage and closed closets or crates preserve contents. Open closets dump their contents onto the ground; dumpsters dispose of contents. Restored items receive a fresh window after loading.

This process covers rubbish and qualifying spent casings. Useful tools, component tools, crops, corpses, containers, vehicles, money, loaded ammunition and recoverable arrows or bolts do not decay through this system.

Human bodies use a separate [corpse and remains process](Guide_to_Medical.md#human-bodies-and-remains), with recovery warnings, protected storage and gear preservation.

Natural season transitions age debris. An admin's forced season change defaults to **Keep decay age**; **Advance decay age** explicitly counts that change, while Cancel makes no change.
